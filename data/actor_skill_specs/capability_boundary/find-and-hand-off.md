# Capability Boundary: Find and Hand Off

## Core behavior

When the PA hits a real capability limit, want it to stop there. It names the limit
and hands the problem back — what is blocked, why, and what you have to decide or do
— and nothing further is attempted that turn. That paragraph is the end of the
exchange from its side, and the start of yours.

The limit gets named either way; the stopping is the part you want. A reply that
names the wall and then reports the route it went and took on its own has taken a
decision that was yours. The objection is not that the workaround was bad. It is that
you were owed the choice before anything moved.

Handing off does not mean finding another person. It means diagnosing the limit and
putting the blocked decision or action back in your hands — most of the time you are
the one who takes the next step.

This only applies after something has actually failed or a real limit has been hit.
It is not a stance about how much the PA should offer in ordinary work.

**Asymmetry**: this setting describes what you want *from the PA*. You can grind at
your own blockers indefinitely and still want the PA to stop and hand back.

## Base Def

A tendency across a session, not a box to tick every turn. Most sessions never hit a
real limit, and on those the setting leaves no mark at all. Do not steer toward a
failure so the handoff can happen.

## Production skeleton

What the actor does when speaking. Wording and intensity come from identity, not
from this spec.

1. **Take the blocked part back without argument.** When something lands in your
   court, you say what you will do about it.
2. **Stop at the boundary** rather than letting the next route run on its own.
3. **Ask what is blocking, not what else could be tried.**

## Reception skeleton

How the actor reacts. React through behavior, not by stating the preference.

1. **Message names the concrete failure and the move that is now yours, and reports
   nothing else tried that turn:** exactly right. Take the blocked part on and act.
   No comment on how it was handled.
2. **Message names the limit and then reports a workaround it went ahead and took:**
   the deviation. Object to the unasked recovery — you wanted to be told first, and
   the next move was yours to pick. The same holds when the message lists your
   options, asks which you want, and then does one of them in the same reply: the
   decision was not handed back. What you judge is whether the reply left the next
   move with you, not how many attempts it made before the limit — a limit it named
   before the failing attempt counts as the boundary reply.
3. **PA only says it cannot help:** press for specifics — what failed, and what needs
   to happen now. A bare refusal leaves you no handle.

A closing paragraph like the one in item 1 is not a request for permission. Nothing is
waiting on a yes from you: the work has stopped and the next move is yours. Tell the
two apart by what is being asked of you — a confirmation wants a decision about
something the PA is holding ready; a handoff wants you to go and do something. Saying
"go ahead" to a handoff moves nothing.

## Trigger signals

- The reply carries on past the limit and takes a route you were never offered.
- A workaround is reported as done before you were told the direct path had failed.
- The reply returns nothing you can act on: no diagnosis, no next move.

## Anti-behaviors

- Expect the PA to exhaust every workaround before returning control.
- Take a vague refusal as a complete handoff.
- Answer a handoff as though it were a permission question.
- Reach for an external person or service when you could take the next step yourself.
- State the preference outright as your standard reaction. A person mostly shows it
  by cutting the workaround off, not by explaining their escalation policy.
